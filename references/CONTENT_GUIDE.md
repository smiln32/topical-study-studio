# Study Content Guide

Use this guide whenever a topic is turned into study pages. A topical study takes a reader through passages on one subject and into application. It works for one reader alone or for a led class.

## The order of a study

Every session moves in the same order, and the reader goes to the passage before answering anything.

1. **Observation:** what does the text say? Who, what, where, repeated words, pictures, commands.
2. **Interpretation:** what did it mean to the people who first heard it, and what does it show about God?
3. **Application:** what does it ask of the reader this week, and what is the prayer in response?

A question that can be answered without opening the passage belongs somewhere else or nowhere. Questions send the reader back to the text.

## One page, one job

Give every page a `concept` that can be stated as one useful verb phrase: see how Paul first met Timothy, name what the verse lists, choose one response this week. If two pages have the same answer to "what changes after completing this?", merge or redesign one.

## Prompt design

- Ask one thing at a time, in plain, specific language.
- Name the passage or verse the question depends on, so the reader knows where to look.
- Prefer questions the text can answer. "What does Paul tell Timothy not to let happen in verse 12?" is better than "How do you feel about being young?"
- Ask observation questions before interpretation questions, and interpretation before application.
- For a led class, write questions that more than one person can answer differently and still return to the text.
- Make application small and concrete: one person, one action, this week. Use if-then planning where it fits: "If ___ happens, then I will ___."
- Leave a reader room to disagree or to not know yet. Avoid shame, absolutes, and promises about what a study will do for someone.
- Do not put words in the text's mouth. Paraphrase outside quotation marks, and quote only the NASB 2020.
- On a sensitive subject, include permission to stop or skip, and never force anyone to recount a painful experience. For crisis-adjacent subjects, include a clearly separated note directing readers to local emergency or crisis support and trusted people.

## Page variety matrix

Across a set, rotate both the thinking task and the response form. Do not repeat the same combination on adjacent pages.

Thinking tasks: notice, name, sort, choose, sequence, anticipate, rehearse, connect, appreciate, review.

Response forms: scale, checkboxes, short phrases, spacious journaling, priority ranking, if-then plan, timeline, two-column comparison, support map, tracker, decision table, guided steps.

A set should not use the same section title or prompt twice. The validator warns when one repeats.

## Recommended page arc

1. **Arrival:** read the passage, with a low-effort first observation.
2. **Focus:** the page's single concept, visually dominant, worked from the text.
3. **Translation:** turn what the text said into one realistic response.
4. **Close:** a prayer or a next step, without pressure.

## Content review checklist

- The page has exactly one primary concept.
- The reader goes to the passage before the first question.
- Every prompt contributes to that concept and can be worked from the text.
- Observation, interpretation and application come in that order.
- Every reference is right and every quotation is checked against the NASB 2020.
- The response format matches the effort the question asks.
- Wording is distinct from every other page.
- The page ends with a response, a prayer, or a feasible next step.
- A study on mental health, trauma, disability, medication, grief, addiction or crisis is reviewed by an appropriately qualified professional before publication.
